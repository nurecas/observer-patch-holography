What the two panels are

Put a point on the sphere. The Hopf map hands back a circle. Every point of the screen has one, no two of them meet, and together they fill the space.

The right panel is the observer's screen: twelve ports in six antipodal pairs, each pair one dipole, with the six axes drawn as diameters. Everything a neighbor can read off this observer is a reading at one of those twelve places.

The left panel is the preimage. Each port lifts to a circle, and the circle is a whole family of internal states that produce the same public reading. Turn around one of those circles and nothing at the boundary changes at all.

That is the useful part of the picture rather than the pretty part. The axiom's own presentation firewall says that hidden labels, embedding coordinates, materials and memory layouts must not enter protected outputs. Here that stops being a hygiene rule and becomes a fact about the shape of the readout: the readout has fibers, and the fiber is exactly the part no public reading resolves.

The circles are closed, and one of them nearly is not

Each fiber is a great circle in S³ of length exactly 2π. None of them runs off to infinity. Stereographic projection does send exactly one fiber to a straight line, the one passing through the point you project from, and that is the only exception there is. The projection axis here runs through a face centre of the carrier, the deepest hole in the port set, which puts every port a comfortable 37.3774° away from it. So all twelve close. Three of them have a projected radius of about 6 and sweep out of the frame at the default zoom, which is what makes them look open; press fit all and they close.

What a dipole looks like down here

Two Hopf fibers never meet and never vary in how far apart they are: they keep a constant distance everywhere along their length, which is what Clifford parallelism means. That distance is exactly half the angle between the two ports on the screen.

So the six axes, whose ports sit at 63.4349°, give fibers a constant 31.717° apart. A dipole's two ports are antipodal, 180° apart, so its two fibers sit at 90°, everywhere, which is the widest two fibers can be. The ± pairing is not a label written on top of the picture. It is the unique pair of circles at the maximum separation, and selecting a dipole shows it: the two cores, with the fibers over the circles of latitude around that axis filling in as nested tori between them.

Every pair passes through every other

Select a single dipole and watch the two circles. They do not touch and they cannot be pulled apart. That holds for any two of the twelve, not just the antipodal ones: each pair has linking number one, which I computed over all sixty-six pairs by Gauss integral before drawing any of this.

So no port's internal states can be separated from any other port's. Whatever the observer is, it is not a bundle of twelve independent things sitting next to each other. Pull on one and you have hold of all of them.

Six axes, one angle

Strip the geometry away and only the agreement table is left: how strongly each dipole's reading correlates with each other one. No dipole is privileged, so every off-diagonal entry has the same size. The diagonal is blank, because a reading agrees with itself for free.

Computed from the six axes, not typed in. Each entry is the correlation between two dipole readings.

Every off-diagonal magnitude is 0.4472135955, which is 1 / √5, an angle of 63.4349°. The signs are the dipoles' own ±.

This table has no space in it. It is a list of numbers about readings. And the number of dimensions is not something the table assumes, it is something you read off afterwards as its rank, which comes out three. That is not a trick to escape a circle: it is how the corpus itself produces space, as a rank-three Gram quotient of the same port structure.

Where the twelve comes from

Two conditions, both of them only restatements of "assume nothing further." Neither determines its own unknown. Together they have one solution.

Compare twice and return

Comparing through the table twice must bring you back where you started, up to scale. That is the coherence of the dipole's own ±, and it says no direction of comparison is preferred. For the icosahedral table it holds exactly: the table squared is five times the identity. Because the diagonal is blank the entries must cancel, so the two eigenvalues are equal and opposite with equal multiplicities, which forces the readings to number exactly twice the rank.

Nothing more fits

The system is maximal: no further equally-correlated reading can be slipped in. That is Gerzon's classical ceiling on equiangular lines, d(d+1)/2.

n = 2d
n = d(d+1)/2
⇒  2d = d(d+1)/2
⇒  4 = d + 1
⇒  d = 3,  n = 6 axes
⇒  12 ports, 30 seams, 20 faces

No other dimension satisfies both. Two is ruled out because it would need four axes and only three fit. Four would need eight and only six fit. Seven would need fourteen and admits twenty-eight, so it is not maximal. Three is the only place the two conditions meet, and it is the one dimension where a maximally symmetric system exactly saturates the ceiling.

The dimension and the port count turn out to be each other's answer. That is the shape of the two-equation fixed point in section 3 of the corpus's own essay, arriving at the rung it otherwise has to assume.

What this is, and is not

This page is not a reading of the corpus. It is a proposal built in conversation alongside it, and the load-bearing premise, that an observer is an assembly of dipoles, is a choice rather than a result. Everything above follows from that choice; none of it establishes it.

The Hopf layer is the most speculative floor of the three. Reading the fiber as the observer's invisible internal phase is an interpretation of the inverse-port involution, not a derivation of it, and pairing opposite charges spends the one topological invariant that made a single-monopole reading attractive.

The corpus states twelve ports inside A1 as axiom content. If the argument on this page holds, that clause becomes a consequence instead. That would be worth something, and it is not yet a proof.
